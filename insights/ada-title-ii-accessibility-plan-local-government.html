How local governments can plan for ADA Title II web accessibility

A practical guide to the WCAG 2.1 Level AA requirements, updated compliance dates, and a realistic accessibility work plan for public services.

By Shahzaib Ahmad Bajwa, Founder. Published October 24, 2026

Short answer: Public entities covered by the ADA Title II web rule must make covered web content and mobile apps conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA. The Department of Justice extended the compliance dates in 2026: April 26, 2027 for entities with a total population of 50,000 or more, and April 26, 2028 for entities below 50,000 and special district governments. Confirm how the rule applies to your entity and services.

Residents use government websites and apps to pay bills, apply for permits, enroll in programs, read public notices, join meetings, and find emergency information. If a service cannot be used with a keyboard, screen reader, captions, or other assistive technology, residents may be unable to access an essential public service.

The 2024 DOJ rule adopted WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the technical standard. DOJ's April 2026 interim final rule extended the compliance dates by one year. An extension gives teams time to plan, but inventories, procurement, remediation, and retesting still take coordination.

Which ADA Title II deadline applies?

The date depends on the public entity and its population under the rule. Special district governments use the later date. School districts and entities with unusual structures should review DOJ's rule and first-steps guidance to identify the applicable category.

1. Confirm the deadline and assign an owner

Identify the public entity responsible for each website and app. Ask the ADA coordinator, IT, procurement, communications, and legal teams to record the applicable date, its basis, and who owns the work. A shared record prevents different departments from planning against different assumptions.

2. Inventory public services, content, and vendors

List public websites, mobile apps, forms, payment portals, document libraries, meeting platforms, and vendor-hosted services. Record each system's purpose, owner, vendor, resident impact, accessibility evidence, and contract or renewal date. Include services offered through licensing, contracts, or other arrangements.

Start with essential resident journeys such as applying for services, paying fees, registering for programs, reading public notices, participating in meetings, and finding emergency information. Those tasks help prioritize systems with high public impact and long vendor lead times.

3. Test complete tasks with assistive technology

Automated accessibility scans can find some issues, but they cannot confirm that a resident can complete a task. Test representative journeys with keyboard-only navigation and screen readers; include people with disabilities in usability testing where possible. Review page structure, labels, focus order, error recovery, contrast, captions, mobile behavior, and document accessibility.

Use WCAG 2.1 Level AA as the rule's technical standard. Treat automated tools as one input to an evaluation, not as an accessibility certification.

4. Prioritize fixes by resident impact and reuse

Repair shared templates and components first so a single fix can improve many pages. Then address high-impact services and frequently used documents. Give each issue an owner, target date, and acceptance criteria. Retest the original user task after each change.

Some content may qualify for a specific exception under the rule, but do not assume an entire class of older documents is exempt. Check DOJ guidance for the content and service in question.

5. Include accessibility in procurement

Ask vendors for current accessibility conformance information, test evidence, known issues, remediation plans, and a contact for reported barriers. Put accessibility requirements, testing access, remediation timelines, and defect-response expectations into new contracts and renewals. A vendor's conformance statement is useful evidence, but it does not replace testing the public service.

For custom software, include acceptance criteria for keyboard access, screen-reader behavior, accessible forms, captions, and document outputs. Test in a non-production environment whenever possible.

6. Make accessibility part of routine operations

Assign responsibilities across IT, communications, procurement, and the ADA coordinator. Train content editors, add accessibility checks to design and release workflows, maintain a clear way for residents to report access barriers, and track issues through retesting. Routine checks help prevent new content and software releases from reintroducing problems.

A practical first month

  1. Week 1: Confirm the entity's deadline, name an accountable lead, and gather website, app, and vendor owners.
  2. Week 2: Inventory public services and contracts; flag essential resident tasks and long vendor lead times.
  3. Week 3: Test a small set of high-impact journeys with keyboard and screen-reader workflows.
  4. Week 4: Build a costed remediation backlog, assign owners, and schedule procurement and retesting.

Frequently asked questions

What technical standard does the Title II web rule use?

The rule adopts Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 Level AA for covered web content and mobile apps, subject to the rule's terms and exceptions.

When must a city or county comply?

Under DOJ's April 2026 extension, the date is April 26, 2027 for covered public entities with a total population of 50,000 or more, and April 26, 2028 for entities below 50,000 and special district governments. Check DOJ's guidance for your entity.

Are automated accessibility scans enough?

No. Automated scans find some detectable issues; keyboard, screen-reader, and usability testing help reveal whether people can complete real tasks.

Does hiring a web vendor transfer responsibility?

No. The rule addresses content and services provided directly or through contractual, licensing, or other arrangements. Public entities should include accessibility expectations in procurement and verify important user journeys.

Is this legal advice?

No. This article is general planning information. Review DOJ's official materials and consult counsel about how the rule applies to your entity.
